Using these words in the room

Knowing the terms gets you through the screen. Using them to explain a decision is what gets you hired. Seniors listen for three things: did you frame the problem, did you name the trade-off, and how will you know it worked.

Answering a design challenge

  1. Clarify the brief“Before I start, who is this for, and what’s the business goal behind it?”
  2. Pick one user and their context“I’ll focus on first-time users on a budget Android phone, one-handed, on 4G.”
  3. Frame the problemTurn the brief into a problem statement or a job to be done. Say it out loud.
  4. Define success“The North Star is X. My input metric is Y, and my guardrail is Z.”
  5. Explore widely, then narrowSketch three different directions quickly. Don’t marry the first.
  6. Choose, and name the trade-off“I picked B. It’s faster for repeat users, at the cost of discoverability for new ones.”
  7. Design the core flow and its statesHappy path first, then empty, loading, error and edge cases. This is where you stand out.
  8. Measure and iterate“I’d ship behind a flag to 10% and watch completion and refund rate for two weeks.”

Walking through a case study

  1. Context and your roleTeam, timeline, what you personally owned. Say “I” for your work, “we” for the team’s.
  2. The problem, with a number“40% of users dropped at address entry.”
  3. What you learnedThe research and the one insight that changed the direction.
  4. Decisions and trade-offsTwo or three key decisions, each with its reason and what you gave up.
  5. What shippedThe flow and its states, not just the hero screen.
  6. OutcomeThe metric that moved. If it’s a concept project, say how you would measure it.
  7. What you’d do differentlyHonest reflection reads as senior. Defensiveness reads as junior.

Say this instead

Sounds juniorSounds senior
“I made it more intuitive.”“Task success went from 2 of 5 to 5 of 5 after we moved refunds into order details.”
“Users will love it.”“My hypothesis is that saved addresses cut drop-off. We’ll know from step completion in two weeks.”
“I made it clean and minimal.”“I cut each screen to one primary action to reduce cognitive load on a small screen.”
“I followed best practices.”“I kept the platform’s checkout convention so people don’t have to learn something new.”
“The PM wanted it.”“The business goal was order value. I balanced it with a guardrail on cancellations.”
“I designed the screens.”“I designed the flow, its empty, loading and error states, and the handoff with annotations.”
“It looks better now.”“The hierarchy now leads with the amount, which is what users check first.”
“I don’t know.”“I haven’t worked with that directly. Here’s how I’d approach it.”
“We did user research.”“I ran six interviews and a tree test. The tree test changed where refunds live.”

Questions to ask them

  1. Which metric is this team measured on this year?
  2. How do design, product and engineering decide when they disagree?
  3. Is there a design system, and who owns it?
  4. How does research reach the team: dedicated researchers, or designers?
  5. What would a great first 90 days look like in this role?
